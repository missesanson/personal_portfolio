## Folding the four headers into connected reasoning — a checklist making one continuous argument is the kind of structure this piece would flag in someone else's writing:

Take this sentence:

> Due to the fact that modern students are now exposed to a very large amount of information from many different sources online, it is becoming increasingly important in today's world for schools to teach critical thinking skills because otherwise students may not be able to know whether the information that they are seeing is reliable, biased, inaccurate or potentially misleading in some way.

The point is sound. The sentence isn't earning it — several claims are stacked into one breathless clause, and "reliable, biased, inaccurate, potentially misleading" is really one idea wearing four costumes.

Tightened, it becomes:

> Students now encounter more information than they can reasonably verify. Schools should therefore teach critical thinking as a practical skill: how to test a source, recognise bias, and distinguish uncertainty from misinformation.

Each change here is doing a specific job, not just a stylistic one. "Due to the fact that" — four words that exist only to delay the sentence — becomes a direct statement of the situation, so the reader starts with the problem instead of a run-up to it. "Critical thinking skills," which could mean almost anything coming out of a teacher's mouth, gets named: testing a source, spotting bias, telling uncertainty from misinformation. And the original's list of near-synonyms collapses into three actual categories, which is what turns repetition into structure. The rhythm improving is really a side effect of those three changes rather than a fourth one: once the sentence has an actual claim instead of a vague one, it naturally splits into two jobs — stating the problem, then stating the response — and that's where the breathing room comes from.

Pushed toward a stronger editorial voice, the same idea could read:

> The internet does not merely give students answers; it gives them claims competing to look like answers. Education must teach them how to inspect those claims — who made them, what evidence supports them, and how confidently they should be believed.

More memorable, but also a different piece of writing — it takes a side rather than describing a situation. Whether that trade is worth making depends on what the piece is for.

None of this is really about finding a fancier sentence. It's about finding the one that does its job with the least friction in the way.
