Before I tell you anything else, I want you to guess something.

It's March 2020. Nobody in this room has ever said the words "social distancing" out loud before. Here's my question: what do you think it actually took to get hundreds of millions of people, across dozens of countries, speaking dozens of languages, to change how they lived almost overnight?

Go on, actually guess. Don't reach for your phone — I promise this pays off faster than a search would.

Most people guess a government order. Some guess a death toll climbing on the news. A few guess a press conference — a minister in a suit, delivering terrible numbers in a very calm voice.

Here's what I think it actually was. It was a picture. Two curves sitting on the same axis — one tall and sharp, one low and wide, both covering exactly the same area — and three words underneath it: *Flatten the curve.*

Nothing about the disease changed that week. Hospital capacity didn't change. Epidemiologists had understood exponential growth since long before any of us were born — you did a version of this math in secondary school, it is not new science. What changed is that a hard, true idea finally found a shape simple enough to survive being passed from person to person, hundreds of millions of times, in a hundred languages, without losing what actually mattered about it.

So here's the question I want to spend the next few minutes on with you. What if the thing that decides whether a true idea changes the world was never really about how true it is?

---

I first felt this personally in my second year of university. There was a professor in one of my classes who was, by every account, brilliant — the kind of mind other academics cited a little nervously. And for three months, I sat in his lectures and understood almost nothing.

For a while, I assumed that meant something about me. I remember genuinely wondering whether I simply wasn't built for the subject — whether everyone else in that hall had some switch in their brain I'd been born without. It took me embarrassingly long to notice that roughly two hundred other people wore the exact same glazed, polite, slightly panicked look I did, every single week. So either that entire class had been unusually slow that semester, or something else was going on.

Here's what I think was actually going on. Every sentence he said had five more sentences buried inside it that he had simply forgotten to say out loud. He wasn't hiding the idea from us. He had lost the map back to where the rest of us were standing.

There's a name for this, and it is not arrogance. Psychologists call it the curse of knowledge, and you have felt its effects even if nobody ever gave you the term for it. Ask a brilliant researcher to explain their life's work in plain language, and what you'll often get back is a wall of vocabulary that took them a decade to build and takes you zero seconds to get lost inside. It is not a character flaw. It is a kind of amnesia. Once you truly know something, it becomes almost impossible to remember what it felt like not to.

Some people make it their life's work to fight that amnesia on purpose. The physicist Richard Feynman built an entire teaching career around refusing to forget what confusion feels like. People still read his Caltech lectures today — not for the physics, you can get the physics from a hundred textbooks — but for the discipline underneath them. A rubber sheet standing in for the curvature of space. An ant walking a tightrope standing in for a wave. An ordinary kitchen-table object doing work no equation alone could do. He treated the hunt for the right analogy with exactly the seriousness most people reserve for the hunt for the right proof.

The statistician Hans Rosling did the same thing, for the deadliest statistics in public health. He'd stand on a stage in front of some of the most educated people in the world and ask them to guess simple facts — how many children die before their fifth birthday, how family size changes as nations develop — and he'd watch rooms full of professors and CEOs score worse than random chance. Worse than chimpanzees picking answers out of a hat. He never lectured people out of being wrong. He let them catch themselves being wrong, live, in public, and then handed them the real number right when they wanted it most. That's the guess I opened with tonight. I stole it from him on purpose.

Most fields don't do any of this. Most treat the translation of an idea as an afterthought, if they treat it as anything at all.

---

You can watch exactly what happens when nobody does that work, in a document almost everyone agrees to and almost nobody reads. Be honest — when did you last actually finish one of these? A widely cited study found that something like nine in ten people click "agree" on a company's terms of service without reading a single word of it, and the number gets worse, not better, the younger you go. This is not laziness. This is arithmetic. By some estimates, reading Microsoft's terms of service start to finish would take you longer than reading the entire *Art of War* — and Sun Tzu, at least, gives you something useful by the end. Somewhere in that document sits the actual, binding shape of your relationship with the phone in your pocket right now. It is technically available to you. In any way that matters, you do not have access to it at all.

It does not have to stay this way. In 2010, the United States passed something called the Plain Writing Act — a law that simply requires federal agencies to write public documents in language ordinary people can use. No more benefits forms that seem engineered to make you give up halfway through. It is a small, unglamorous law, and it has still changed how tens of millions of people interact with their own government. Nothing about the underlying rules changed. What changed was who could actually reach them.

So if plain language is this powerful, why does it still feel like the boring cousin of real work? Why do we, instinctively, trust the complicated version more than the clear one?

---

This is not a new problem, and it was never a small one. For most of European history, the single most consequential text in Western civilization existed only in a language ordinary people did not speak. Latin. Readable by clergy. Unreadable by the people whose entire lives it governed — their marriages, their laws, their fate after death, all written down in words they were never taught.

In the 1520s, a scholar named William Tyndale decided to translate the New Testament into English anyway. As the story is told, he once said to a learned clergyman who'd argued people were better off without scripture in their own tongue, that he'd make it so a boy who drove a plough would know more of it than the man standing in front of him. Church authorities did not treat that as a stylistic preference. They hunted him across Europe for it. In 1536, they caught him. He was strangled, and then his body was burned at the stake, for the crime of making a hard idea reachable.

Good translation does not just make an idea easier to find. It changes who is allowed to hold it. That is exactly why it has always been contested — and exactly why, in quieter ways, it still is.

---

Here's the part that should worry us more than it worried anyone in Tyndale's time. We are drowning in more information than any human being could read in ten lifetimes, and inside all that noise, the ideas that spread are not, by default, the accurate ones. They are the well-told ones. Put a complicated truth that needs five minutes and a diagram to land in a room next to a simple, confident, wrong answer that takes five seconds, and the wrong answer wins that room almost every time. That is not a character flaw we get to shame people out of. It is a design constraint. And any field that complains about being misunderstood by the public owes itself one honest question: how much of that gap did we try to close ourselves, and how much did we just leave lying there for someone far less careful to fill in first?

I want to be clear about something, because it's the part people get wrong the moment they hear a talk like this one. None of this is an argument for dumbing anything down. There is a real difference between the complexity that belongs to an idea and the difficulty that gets bolted onto it afterward — by jargon nobody bothered to translate, by an explanation that follows the order the writer discovered the idea in instead of the order a reader can actually absorb it in, by a simple unwillingness to go looking for the second metaphor instead of settling, tired, for the first one. Good writing does not remove the hard part of a hard idea. It removes the parts that were never actually load-bearing in the first place. What is left is simpler. It is not smaller. And getting there is usually harder than the original discovery was, not easier.

Done right, simplicity is not a concession to your reader. It is the single greatest act of respect you can pay them.

---

So let me go back to the guess I asked you to make.

You probably did not guess "a picture." Most people don't, because we've been trained, our whole lives, to believe that finding an idea and explaining an idea are the same job, done by the same kind of person. They are not. Somebody had to sit with a hard, technical, true idea about exponential curves — the same math my professor could have explained to you for three hours without you retaining a single sentence of it — and translate it down into a shape that a tired, frightened, distracted public could actually hold onto. That translation was not a lesser form of the discovery. In March 2020, it may have saved more lives than the discovery did.

We already know how to reward the person who finds the truth. We give them the lab, the tenure, the Nobel Prize, the footnote in the textbook. We have never been nearly as serious about rewarding the person who finds the sentence. The diagram. The three words that let everyone else hold what the first person found.

Tyndale paid for that job with his life. Most people who do it today just pay for it with a lack of credit — a missing line on a CV, a quiet, shared assumption that what they do is somehow less than the "real" work. I don't think that's a small mistake. I think it might be the mistake — the one standing between us and every idea we already have, right now, that could still change something, if only somebody were willing to do the unglamorous work of making it reachable.

The next pandemic. The next climate report. The next hard idea that actually matters, sitting in a paper somewhere, true and correct and almost completely useless, because nobody has found its plain English yet. Somewhere between the discovery and the headline, someone is going to have to draw the curve, and find the three words that make it travel.

I hope, when that moment comes, we're finally serious enough to let them.

## Note to the speaker

I built the opening around Hans Rosling on purpose — not just as an example inside the talk, but as the actual model for how the whole piece works. Rosling was a physician and statistician who spent his career making the deadliest, most misunderstood numbers in public health legible to ordinary rooms. He didn't lecture people out of their misconceptions. In his talks — start with "The Best Stats You've Ever Seen" (2006) and the audience-quiz format he later refined in "How Not to Be Ignorant About the World" — he quizzed the room first, let them get it wrong together, and only then handed them the real number. That's the same move this talk makes on you in the first thirty seconds. He was also, genuinely, a sword-swallower, a skill he taught himself in medical school after a professor used an X-ray of a sword-swallower's throat to explain anatomy, and he closed one of his TED talks by swallowing a sword on stage. Even his party trick was a lesson in how a plain demonstration beats an abstract explanation. He's worth watching directly, not just reading about.

The professor story is a placeholder built to show the shape a personal story needs — specific enough to feel lived-in, with a real turn in it. It isn't your real memory, so it won't land the way your real one will. Swap in the actual class, the actual thing that confused you, the actual moment you realized it wasn't your fault. The details you actually remember will out-perform anything I can invent for you. A true, slightly embarrassing memory always beats a polished, generic one.

The speech itself runs about 1,980 words, which lands around 13 to 15 minutes at a natural pace that leaves room for the pauses and the laughs. TED's own famous rule of thumb is 18 minutes, even for speakers given longer slots — the discipline of stopping before you've used all the time is usually what makes a talk feel tight rather than long. You have thirty minutes to work with, which means you have room to slow down, not an obligation to fill it. If you do want to stretch it further, the two natural places to expand are the personal anecdote (once it's your real story, it can probably carry another minute or two) and the Tyndale section, which could easily support a second historical example — the printing press and Luther's German Bible is the obvious parallel if you want one.

Three places want real silence, not just a paragraph break: right after "Flatten the curve" near the top, right after "so either that entire class had been unusually slow that semester, or something else was going on" — let the laugh land before you explain the curse of knowledge — and right after "he was strangled, and then his body was burned at the stake." Say that line plainly, without hurrying past it, and let it sit for a second before you move on.

If you want to keep pulling on this thread, Chris Anderson's *TED Talks: The Official TED Guide to Public Speaking* is the clearest single book on what actually makes a talk like this work, and Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* is the best breakdown of the structural pattern this draft is built on — a real "what is" set against a hopeful "what could be," over and over, until the ending.
